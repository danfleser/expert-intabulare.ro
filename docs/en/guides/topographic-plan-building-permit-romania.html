Where surveying enters the permit chain

Every authorized construction in Romania walks the same statutory chain, set by the construction law (Law 50/1991): urbanism certificate → approvals and design → building permit (autorizație de construire) → execution → final reception. If you are used to permitting in Germany, the UK or the US, the sequence will feel familiar; what differs is the vocabulary and the specific technical documents each step demands. Surveying appears at three points, and weak or missing survey pieces are one of the most common reasons permit files bounce back from Romanian town halls.

First: the site plan on OCPI-endorsed topographic support. Required from the urbanism certificate stage onward and again in the technical documentation for the permit itself (called DTAC), this is a measured plan of the real terrain — boundaries, neighbours, roads, existing buildings, utilities — drawn at the scales the regulations require (usually 1:500 or 1:1000) and formally received by OCPI, the county cadastre office. The OCPI endorsement (viza) is what makes it official rather than a sketch; town halls will not accept an unendorsed drawing.

Second: the topographic study for the designers. Your architect and structural engineer cannot design responsibly without elevation data — spot heights, contour lines, slopes, and the exact position of boundaries and any networks. On sloped Transylvanian plots this study decides earthworks volumes and often the whole positioning of the house.

Third: the stakeout. When execution begins, the designed building footprint is set out on the ground from project coordinates, with a formal stakeout report. This is the guarantee that what gets built stands where it was authorized — a condition checked again at final reception.

And the condition before all conditions: the land must be registered. No land book, no permit. If your plot is unregistered or the registration is outdated, that is stage zero of the project plan.

Survey documents a town hall or designer may require

  • Site plan on cadastral/topographic support, received (endorsed) by OCPI — for the urbanism certificate and the DTAC file
  • Topographic study with contours and spot heights for design — essential on sloped plots
  • Zoning location plan (plan de încadrare în zonă) at the scale the urbanism certificate specifies
  • A recent land registry extract for the plot
  • Construction stakeout report at the start of execution
  • As-built survey and updated cadastral documentation at completion, to register the finished building

What foreign owners get wrong — and how to sequence it well

The classic mistakes we see from abroad: buying a plot and commissioning design work before checking the land is registered and the geometry matches reality; assuming the site plan from the seller's old file is still valid (town halls want current measurements, and boundaries have often been re-registered since); and leaving the stakeout to the builder's tape measure — the cheapest way to end up with a house encroaching a setback or a neighbour's strip. Romanian setback rules and urbanism-certificate conditions are enforced against measured positions, not good intentions.

The efficient sequence for a remote client: we verify the registration and measure the plot once, thoroughly — one field mobilization yields the OCPI-endorsed site plan, the design-stage topographic study, and the boundary verification. Your architect designs on our data; when the permit is issued, we return for the stakeout, and at the end for the as-built and the registration of the finished building, without which the house legally does not exist and cannot be sold or mortgaged. Frequently asked questions

How long does the topographic documentation take?

The measurement itself is typically a single field day; drafting takes a few working days; the OCPI reception of the site plan adds its statutory processing term. Plan one to three weeks for the survey package — start it in parallel with the urbanism certificate application, not after.

Can I use a survey plan from a few years ago?

Usually not. Town halls and designers expect current data, and reality changes — new neighbouring registrations, moved fences, new utilities. Reusing stale plans is a false economy that surfaces as a rejected file months later.

Do you work with foreign architects?

Yes. We deliver the topographic study in standard digital formats (DWG/DXF with coordinates and elevations) that any design office can use, and we coordinate directly with your designer in English. Note that the permit file itself must be signed by professionals authorized in Romania — your foreign architect typically partners with a local one.

What does the survey package cost relative to the project?

A fraction of one percent of any serious construction budget. Ask us with the plot's land book number and your project outline, and we quote the whole package — site plan, study, stakeout, as-built — so there are no surprises mid-project. See also our general pricing guide.
